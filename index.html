Module 00

How to use this system

Fifteen modules. Read 00–02 once. Train from 03–11. Live in 12–14. Everything else is reference you come back to.

The three things that change everything

1
Every session ends in football.Technique first, but the last 10–15 minutes of every session are at speed, under a constraint, with a decision and an end product (shot, cross, cutback). A drill that never becomes football is a hobby.
2
Shooting is the default in the zone.Inside the box with sight of goal, or in your cut-in lane with the defender more than a stride off you: you shoot. A pass from the zone needs a reason. A shot doesn't. This is a rule, not a mood.
3
Mistakes get five seconds.Lose the ball → breathe out → cue word → next action (press, run, show). Nothing else is allowed to happen in your head in that window. Confidence is built from repeating this, not from playing well.

Kit you need

  • 3+ balls (solo shooting and crossing dies with one ball)
  • 8–12 flat cones and 2–4 tall cones or poles
  • A wall or rebounder — your passing, receiving and first-time-finishing partner
  • A goal, or two poles/jumpers on a fence or wall with a target zone marked
  • A "defender": mannequin, bag, bin, or a school bag on a pole — anything with height
  • Your phone on a fence — filming 5 minutes of a session and 20 minutes of a match is worth more than any drill in this document
  • Skipping rope, resistance band for the physical module

Sessions are written for 45–60 minutes because that's what a school week allows. Two focused hours beat six distracted ones.

Honesty rule
The ratings in Module 01 are yours. Every six weeks you re-test (Module 14) and re-rate. The system updates from evidence, not feelings. A bad match is a data point, not a verdict.

Module 01

Profile & player identity

Your self-assessment, read like a scout would read it — then turned into a plan. The pattern is unmistakable: an explosive athlete with a good football brain for movement, whose technique under speed and pressure hasn't caught up with the body yet, and whose confidence is being set by outcomes instead of process.

What a scout sees

Standout trait: 10/10 acceleration with 7/10 off-ball movement and instinct for the back post. That combination is rare and it is exactly what modern wide players are bought for — it is the thing to protect and build around.

The gap that defines the next 12 months: ball carrying at speed 2/10 next to acceleration 10/10. You have a Ferrari engine bolted to a bicycle's steering. Closing that gap alone changes what kind of player you are.

The invisible ceiling: confidence 2/10, handling mistakes 3/10, consistency 3/10 — with concentration and competitiveness both 10/10. That is not a soft player. That is a player who cares so much the outcome controls him. The fix is process rules and reps, not pep talks.

Position fit: right-footed on the left is the inverted-winger profile: cut inside onto the strong foot to shoot or switch, go outside with the weak foot or outside of the right to cross. Your "ping to the far side after beating one" is already a textbook inverted-winger action.

Identity statement

Read before every session and match

I am an explosive, aggressive, intelligent left winger. I press first. I run in behind before anyone else has thought about it. I take the full-back on. I shoot in the zone. I arrive at the back post. When I lose the ball I win it back or I make the next run. I influence the game even when the team is struggling, because my actions don't depend on the score.

"Skill merchant" is banned. Every move in this system has a purpose and an end product.

Module 02

The development model

Every skill in this system climbs the same eight rungs. You don't skip rungs, and you don't stay on one longer than it takes to earn the next. Solo training can reach rung 5 honestly. Rungs 6–8 need people — that's what team training, small-sided games and matches are for.

TechniqueSlow, clean, repeatable. Both feet.
MovementSame action while moving, from different angles.
SpeedMatch tempo. Still clean.
PressureTime limit, space limit, chased, fatigued.
DecisionTwo or more options; you choose late.
OpponentPassive → active → live defender.
GameSmall-sided, constraints, scoring.
Match11v11. Track it. Review it.

Solo versions of "pressure", "decision" and "opponent"

Pressure without a defender: a clock (finish before a count of 3), a shrinking space (cones 1 m closer each set), fatigue (10 s sprint before the action), or consequence (miss = 5 press-ups, and you keep score honestly).

Decision without a coach: randomise the cue. Number your cones or targets, call a number out loud as late as possible, or use a phone timer that beeps at random intervals. Bounce the ball off an uneven wall so the return is unpredictable. The point is you don't know the answer before the action starts.

Opponent without a person: a mannequin gives you a fixed reference to attack the front foot of. It is not a defender. Be honest in your log: "vs mannequin" ≠ "vs 1v1". Get real 1v1s at school breaks, at team training, with a mate, with a sibling — even a passive body changes the picture.

How to know you've earned the next rung

  • Technique → Movement: 8/10 clean at slow speed, both feet.
  • Movement → Speed: no drop in quality when moving from three different angles.
  • Speed → Pressure: quality holds at match tempo for 3 sets.
  • Pressure → Decision: quality holds under a clock/space/fatigue constraint.
  • Decision → Opponent: you pick the right option 8/10 with a late cue.
  • Opponent → Game: works vs an active defender more than half the time.
  • Game → Match: you did it in a small-sided game without thinking about it.

Drills below carry a ladder strip showing which rungs they cover. Combine drills in a session so you climb, e.g. 10 min rung 1–2, 15 min rung 3–4, 15 min rung 5–6.

Crossing (3/10 → usable)

You are right-footed on the left, so your crossing options are different from a left-footer's and you should train them as three distinct deliveries rather than "crossing":

  1. The cutback (right foot, from the byline or the corner of the six-yard box) — your highest-value delivery. Pulled back low to the penalty spot area. Technique: inside of the right foot, hips open to the target, clipped not blasted.
  2. The early whipped cross (right foot, from wide-left, 25–30 m out) — inswinging towards the back post. The ball curls away from the keeper. This suits your "cut inside slightly then deliver" body shape.
  3. The left-foot driven/low cross from the line — needed when you've gone outside the full-back at pace. Low, hard, between the keeper and defenders. Accuracy over shape.

Priority order is exactly that: 1, then 2, then 3. A 15-year-old inverted winger who owns the cutback and the inswinger is already dangerous.

Module 04

Dribbling & 1v1

You don't need ten moves. You need two you can do at full speed in either direction, a change of pace that makes both of them work, and the eyes to know which one the defender is asking for.

The 1v1 principle for a fast player

With 10/10 acceleration your 1v1 game is not about tricking the defender — it's about creating a moment where they're flat-footed and you're already moving. Every move below is a way to put the defender's weight on the wrong foot for one step. One step is all you need.

Attack the front foot. Drive straight at the defender's leading foot at pace. They must either turn (you go past on the side they turned away from) or plant (you go past on the side of the planted foot, because it can't push off). The move happens at 1.5–2 m from the defender, not 5 m.

Read the body: hips open showing you inside → go outside. Square hips → attack the front foot and go either way. Backing off at speed → no move needed, run at them until they stop, then move. Standing tall/flat-footed → stop-start.

Module 05

Ball carrying at speed

The 2/10 next to the 10/10. This module has one job: make the ball travel as fast as you do. It's a technical skill with three parts — touch length, touch surface, and eyes — and it's trained separately before it's combined.

The touch-length rule

Open space (nearest defender 8 m+): big touches, one every 3–4 strides, ball pushed 4–6 m ahead with the laces or the outside of the foot. Run through the ball, don't chase it.

Approaching a defender (5–8 m): medium touches, every 2 strides, ball 2 m ahead. Eyes come up to read the defender's body.

Engaging (under 3 m): small touches, every stride, ball within one stride. This is where Module 04 takes over.

Your current 2/10 is almost certainly a touch-length problem: either too short (you keep stopping to touch the ball) or too long and uncontrolled (the ball runs away). The drills fix that by making touch length a conscious variable.

Surfaces at speed

  • Laces (straight carry): toe down, push with the top of the foot mid-stride. The most efficient carry.
  • Outside of the foot (curving carry / change of direction): lets you bend your run without breaking stride. Outside-right carries you inside from the left wing — your natural line.
  • Inside of the foot at speed: only for a sharp cut; it costs you a stride.
  • Never the sole at full speed unless you're stopping on purpose.

Protecting the ball at speed: carry it on the foot away from the recovering defender, slightly across your body, with your inside arm as a barrier.

Module 06

Shooting & finishing

Two problems, two solutions. The technique problem gets reps with clear cues. The decision problem — passing when you should shoot — gets a rule, a zone map, and scenarios that make you choose before you know the answer.

Striking clean — the cues that matter

Laces (power / driven): plant foot beside the ball, a hand's width away, toe at the target. Toe down, ankle locked like a hammer. Strike through the centre or slightly above centre to keep it down. Head still and over the ball. Land on the kicking foot — that means you struck through it.

Inside of the foot (placement / across the keeper): open the hips, plant foot slightly behind and beside the ball, strike the middle with the inside of the boot, follow through towards the far post. This is the finish for 70% of your chances as a winger — cutbacks, back post, the cut-in curler.

The cut-in curler (inside of the right foot, wrapping): body opens to the far post, strike across the ball from outside to inside, follow through around it. Aim for the far post at ground level and let the curl bring it in. Inverted-winger signature.

Near-post drive (left foot, or outside-right): from the byline side. Hard and low across the keeper's near side. Placement matters less than speed and height (under the crossbar, above the ground).

Your finishing zones

Where a left winger actually scores from. Train these, not random shots from 25 m.

  • Zone A — the cut-in lane: left of the D, 16–20 m out, after cutting inside onto the right foot. Finish: far-post curler or a driven low shot near post if the keeper cheats.
  • Zone B — the second six-yard box (back post): 6–10 m out, arriving on a cross or cutback from the right. Finish: first-time inside foot, side-foot volley, or a controlled second touch and finish.
  • Zone C — the cutback receiver: penalty spot / edge of the six-yard box, ball rolling across you from the byline. Finish: first-time, inside foot, across the keeper, low.
  • Zone D — in behind, 1v1 with the keeper: from your run in behind. Finish: early, low, across the keeper's far side, before they set. Or a touch round them if they come out fast.
  • Zone E — the byline angle: tight angle after going outside. Finish: near-post drive (left foot) or, usually better, the cutback. Know the difference.

The shoot rule

In the zone, I shoot. A pass from the zone needs a reason.

Shoot when

  • Inside the box with sight of goal — any sight.
  • Zone A and the defender is more than a stride away as you cut.
  • 1v1 with the keeper — early, before they set.
  • The ball drops to you with no defender between you and the goal inside 20 m.
  • You've hesitated once already this match. The next one goes.

The only valid reasons to pass from the zone

  • A teammate has an open goal or a tap-in and you'd be shooting through bodies.
  • A defender is blocking the line and the pass unblocks it (a square ball round the block).
  • The keeper has closed the angle to nothing and a teammate is free centrally.

"Someone might be in a better position" is not on that list. "I might miss" is not on that list.

Module 07

Receiving under pressure & scanning

These are one skill with two halves. Scanning is how you know what the pressure is; receiving is what you do about it. Trained together from day one, because a scan you don't act on is decoration.

The two looks

Look 1 — when the passer looks up. The moment a teammate's head comes up to find you, check over the shoulder nearest the pitch. You want: where is my marker, where is the space.

Look 2 — as the ball travels. Halfway through the pass, one more check. Has the marker moved? Did the space close? This second look is what separates 4/10 from 8/10 scanners; elite midfielders and wide players scan several times in the seconds before receiving.

What you're looking for, in order: (1) nearest defender — distance and direction of approach, (2) space I can take my first touch into, (3) the next pass or run option. Three things, in that order, every time.

Then the picture decides the touch:

  • Marker tight behind → bounce one-touch to the passer or a third man, or set it and spin.
  • Marker 2–3 m behind → half-turn receive on the back foot, first touch away from them, face forward.
  • Marker on your inside → touch outside and go down the line.
  • Marker on your outside / showing you inside → touch inside onto the right and drive at the centre-back or ping.
  • No pressure → big touch forward and carry (Module 05).

Body orientation for a left winger

Receive side-on, left shoulder to the touchline, hips open to the pitch, so both your right foot (inside) and the run down the line are available. Receiving square to the passer with your back to goal is the picture that leaves you "stuck" — it's where your 3/10 lives.

Back foot = the foot furthest from the ball — for you on the left wing, usually the right foot. Receiving on the back foot lets the ball run across your body and your first touch is already forward. That single habit changes most of your receptions.

Timing the movement: come short at the moment the passer can play it, not before. Two steps towards the ball as it's struck creates the metre you need. Double movements are in Module 09.

Module 08

Football IQ Lab

Decision-making trained the way it works in a match: you see a picture, you commit, then you find out. Twelve scenarios below — pick before you reveal. Your score is saved on this device. Re-run them weekly until every one is automatic, then send me match clips and I'll build new ones from your own games.

Scan→See→Anticipate→Decide→Execute

What each step means for you

  • Scan — the two looks (Module 07). Before the ball, not after.
  • See — three things, in order: nearest defender, space, next option.
  • Anticipate — where will the defender be in one second? Where will my teammate be? Play the next picture, not this one.
  • Decide — late but fast. The decision is made on the second-last touch, not while standing still.
  • Execute — with commitment. A 70% decision executed at 100% beats a 100% decision executed at 70%. Hesitation is the worst decision.

Watching homework (20 min, twice a week)

Pick one elite inverted left winger (right-footed on the left) and watch only them for a 20-minute stretch, ball or no ball. Pause when they receive and ask: where did they scan, what was their body shape, what did they do and why? Then watch a wide player who goes outside more, and compare.

Write down three things per session in Module 13's notes. Not "he was good" — "he checked his shoulder twice, received side-on, touched inside when the full-back was outside."

Also watch the full-back marking them. Learn what full-backs hate: early runs in behind, being isolated with no cover, the winger arriving at pace instead of receiving to feet.

Isolating the full-back

The best thing that can happen to you in a match is a 1v1 with the full-back, 30 m from goal, with no cover next to them. You can engineer it:

  • Hold width in build-up so the FB can't get help from the winger ahead of them.
  • Receive on the move, not standing — a standing winger lets the cover arrive.
  • Attack immediately after receiving. Every second you wait, their midfielder gets 2 m closer.
  • If the cover is already there, that's the switch or the cutback pass — the isolation comes next time.

LWB / LB — your secondary jobs

Playing deeper is not a demotion — it's more touches, more receiving under pressure and more running in behind from deep, which is exactly your programme. Adjust two things:

  • Defensive positioning: goal-side of the winger, body open so you see the ball and them. Show them outside. Don't dive in — delay until the cover arrives. Your acceleration makes recovery runs a strength here.
  • Attacking from deep: your run is later and longer. Underlap when the winger holds width; overlap when they come inside. The end product is the low cross or cutback more than the shot.

Module 10

Physical development

Protect the explosiveness, build the engine, and get strong enough to stay on your feet. Age-appropriate: bodyweight and technique first, no maximal lifting without a qualified coach, and any persistent pain gets seen by someone qualified before training continues.

Growth note
At 15 you may be in or just past your fastest growth period. Heel, knee or hip pain during growth is common and is a reason to reduce load, not push through. Speed and strength both dip temporarily when limbs grow — if a test goes backwards for six weeks, that's biology, not lost work.

Stamina (4 → 7)

Two runs a week. Football stamina is repeated high-intensity efforts with short recoveries, not steady jogging.

  • Run 1 — intervals: 4 × 4 min hard (can speak 2–3 words) / 3 min easy jog. Progress to 5 × 4.
  • Run 2 — repeated sprints: 2 sets of 8 × 30 m sprint, 20 s walk back, 4 min between sets. Add a ball to half of them from week four.
  • Every session: the last 10 minutes of ball work is done at match tempo without rest — that's stamina with a ball.

Test: 5-minute run distance, or the beep test if school has it. Re-test every six weeks.

Strength & robustness (4 → 6)

Two 25-minute sessions a week, bodyweight and band. Quality over volume.

  • Lower: split squats 3×8/leg · single-leg glute bridges 3×10/leg · Nordic hamstring lowers 3×4 (sprinters' hamstrings need this) · Copenhagen adductor holds 3×20 s · calf raises 3×15.
  • Trunk: dead bugs 3×10 · side planks 3×30 s · Pallof press with band 3×10/side.
  • Upper (for shielding): press-ups 3×max · inverted rows or band rows 3×12.

Month three onwards: add a light loaded goblet squat and Romanian deadlift only if someone qualified checks your technique first.

Speed, agility & balance

You don't need more acceleration. You need to keep it, add top speed, and be able to use it on one leg while turning.

  • Top speed: once a week, 4 × 40 m flying sprints (20 m build, 20 m fly), full recovery. Tall posture, relaxed face and hands.
  • Agility: 5-10-5 shuttles and reactive cuts (a partner points, you cut) — 8 reps, once a week, fresh.
  • Balance & coordination (your two unknowns): single-leg hops and sticks 3×5/leg, skipping 3 × 1 min, ladder or line hops for 3 min in warm-ups. Test single-leg balance eyes closed: under 10 s = a priority.

Warm-up every session: 8 minutes — jog, dynamic stretches (leg swings, walking lunges, hip openers), 3 accelerating strides.

Module 11

Mind: confidence, composure, consistency

Your confidence is 2/10 because it's being built on outcomes, and outcomes in football are noisy. Rebuild it on process: things you fully control, that you can hit on your worst day. Every match you hit them, the evidence grows. Confidence follows evidence.

The three non-negotiables

Whatever the score, whatever your form, whatever the coach said. These are yours to hit in every match. They are also, not by accident, your identity.

1
Press the first trigger I see in every ten-minute block.Controllable, aggressive, gets you into the game physically before the ball arrives.
2
Make four runs in behind before half-time.Even if none are found. You become a problem the full-back thinks about.
3
Apply the shoot rule the first time I'm in the zone.Miss the target if you must. The rule was kept. That's the win.

After the match you rate only these three (yes/no). A 3/3 on a day the team lost 4–0 and you played badly is a successful match for your development. That's not a trick — it's how academies actually assess young players.

The five-second reset

After any mistake — lost ball, missed chance, bad pass:

  1. Breathe out hard, once. Long exhale.
  2. Cue word. Pick one now and never change it. Examples: "Next." "Go." "Press." Say it in your head or under your breath.
  3. Next action within five seconds: press the ball, make a run, show for a pass, sprint back. A physical action, not a thought.

Practise it in training: every time you lose the ball in a drill, do the reset before touching a ball again. It has to be automatic before it will survive a match.

Why it works: the "scared / hesitating / going quiet" pattern you describe is your attention moving from the pitch to yourself. The reset drags it back onto the pitch. You can't be anxious and sprinting to press at the same time — the body doesn't allow it.

Composure in front of goal

  • Routine beats feeling. Corner → ball → exhale → strike. The same in the composure ladder (Module 06) as in a cup final. The routine is what you fall back on when the feeling isn't there.
  • Decide early, execute late. Most panicked finishes are decided at the last moment. Pick the corner as you enter the zone.
  • Low and hard is never wrong. If the head goes blank: low, hard, across the keeper. Give the goalkeeper something to save. Keepers spill; the back post arrives.
  • Misses are rent. Every finisher pays it. Count shots, not goals, for the first three months. Goals follow shots. They can't follow passes.

Consistency & discipline

  • The minimum session: on a day you can't face it — 15 minutes of wall work and 20 strikes. Never zero. Consistency is built on the bad days.
  • Pre-match routine (same every time): night before — kit ready, read the identity statement, name your three non-negotiables. Matchday — same food, same warm-up additions (10 strikes, 6 accelerations), 60 seconds alone: exhale, cue word, first action planned ("first ball: run in behind").
  • The 24-hour rule: no judgement of a match until the next day. Then the review (Module 13), then done. No replaying it for a week.
  • Selection setbacks: write down what the coach said and what you think they saw. Turn it into one project in this system. Then close the notebook. Information, not identity.
  • Sleep: 8.5–9.5 hours at your age. It is the single biggest performance and consistency lever you have and it's free.

Last word

Nothing in this system asks you to prove anyone wrong. It asks you to be measurably better at ten things in six weeks, and again six weeks after that. Players who do that for two years become players people have to plan for. Whether that ends in professional football isn't something a document can promise — but the player it builds will know exactly how good he is, and why.
